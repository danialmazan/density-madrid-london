From their full-city population density figures, Madrid and London might seem equally dense. But are they?

01

The first impression

The headline numbers are almost a draw.

Divide every resident by every square kilometre inside the administrative boundary and both cities land near 5,700 people per km². That calculation is correct. It is also answering a narrower question than it appears to.

The density spectrum

Madrid’s low average is mostly land where almost nobody lives.

66.4% of Madrid’s territory lies in sections below 1,000 residents per km², but those sections contain only 1.6% of its population. London distributes both land and residents more gradually across the middle of the scale.

06

Population by density band

London clusters low. Madrid spreads high.

Nearly 79% of Londoners live in LSOAs below 15,000 people per km², compared with about 21% of Madrid residents. At the other end, 61% of Madrid residents live in sections at 25,000 or more—against just 3.5% in London.

The useful conclusion

The two cities are not “equally dense.” Their administrative averages merely happen to match.

Method & limits

Comparable, not identical.

Geographies. Madrid municipality uses 2,450 census sections; Greater London uses 4,994 LSOAs across its 33 local authorities. LSOAs were selected because their usual population range of 1,000–3,000 is much closer to Spanish census sections than OAs.

Dates. Madrid population is from the INE Annual Population Census at 1 January 2024. London population is the ONS mid-year estimate at 30 June 2024 on 2021 LSOA boundaries. Both count usual residents, but the systems and reference dates are not identical.

Area. Density is population divided by full polygon area. No parks, water, roads or industrial zones were removed. A section or LSOA is treated as uniformly dense for distribution calculations.

Interpretation. This is residential population density—not built-up density, floor-space density, household crowding, building height or daytime population. Results depend on administrative boundaries and small-area design.
